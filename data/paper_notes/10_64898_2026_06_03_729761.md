Reservoir competence for arenaviruses and hantaviruses appears to track synanthropy, but surveillance has preferentially targeted synanthropic species in high-income regions. Whether that association is biological or an artefact of where sampling has occurred could not be separated without a record of effort as well as of detection.

Using the ArHa database of global sampling records, taxonomic and geographic bias were quantified first. Surveillance is skewed towards the Palearctic and towards widespread, large-bodied species; 46% of rodent and shrew genera remain entirely unsampled after fifty years, and sampling intensity correlates with accessibility and night-light intensity rather than with host biodiversity. Statistically correcting for historical sampling volume, reservoir status is then estimated as a predictable trait: a fast pace of life, meaning early maturity, large litters and small body mass, independently predicts it, and fully synanthropic species show roughly 1.8-fold higher odds of reservoir competence.

Mapping intrinsic biological hazard rather than reactive spillover risk suggests land-use change acts as an ecological filter, with species-poor disturbed landscapes sometimes harbouring communities of higher intrinsic reservoir potential than intact tropical forest.
